# Refining margins 2021–2026: what the crack spreads show

Data: NYMEX/ICE continuous front-month futures (Yahoo Finance), daily closes
4 Jan 2021 – 30 Sep 2026. Crack spreads use WTI as the crude leg; product prices
converted from $/gal to $/bbl (×42). "Complete years" = 2021–2025.

## The finding

Across 2021–2025, the distillate crack averaged **$38.29/bbl in Q4 vs $31.82/bbl in
Q2, $6.47/bbl higher**, and Q4 beat Q2 in four of the five years (2024 was the
exception). The gasoline crack ran the other way, averaging **$31.30/bbl in Q2 and
$17.34/bbl in Q4**, and was above its own annual average in every April–August
of all five years, which makes it the most reliable seasonal pattern in the data.
2026 is well outside that range: the 3-2-1 crack averaged **$65.83/bbl in Q3 2026**,
compared with $27.50 for 2021–2025, and the distillate crack peaked at
**$117.92/bbl on 16 Sep 2026**.

## Why

**Gasoline peaks in spring and summer.** There are three reasons, in order of
importance:
1. *Driving season.* US gasoline demand peaks between Memorial Day and Labor Day.
2. *Spring refinery maintenance.* Refiners schedule turnarounds for February–April,
   between winter heating demand and summer driving, so supply dips just as
   stocks are being built.
3. *Summer-grade specification.* NYMEX RBOB contracts for April–September delivery
   are low-volatility (low-RVP) summer grade, which costs more to make. The
   continuous front-month series switches to summer grade around the March
   roll, so part of the jump from February (−$4.90 vs year average) to March
   (+$4.65) is a spec change rather than a demand change.

**Distillate is strongest in October–November** (+$6.18 and +$4.93 vs the
year's average). The drivers are heating-oil stock building ahead of the US
Northeast winter (NY Harbor is the delivery point), autumn refinery turnarounds
cutting output, harvest-season diesel demand, and Gulf Coast hurricane risk
(August–October). It's a noisier signal than gasoline for two reasons. First,
refiners shift yields toward distillate in winter, which dampens the pattern.
Second, diesel is a global market: after Russian product exports were
sanctioned in 2022–23, European demand pulled US cracks around regardless of
the season.

**The 3-2-1 crack follows gasoline**, because gasoline is two-thirds of the
barrel in the formula: it peaks in May–August and bottoms in December–January.

## Other things the data shows

- **Brent–WTI** averaged $3.90/bbl over 2021–2025, roughly the cost of moving
  crude from Cushing to the Gulf Coast and across the Atlantic. In 2026 it hit
  +$16.97 on 31 Mar and −$3.68 on 7 Apr.
- **Those negative prints are mostly a data artefact.** Continuous front-month
  series don't always compare the same delivery month. Brent's front month
  expires at the end of the month two months before delivery, so on 1 April BZ=F
  rolled to the June contract while CL=F was still on May until about 21 April.
  In a steeply backwardated market, June Brent trades below May WTI. The
  negative days in May 2022 (17–20 May) fall in the same window before a WTI
  expiry. A clean spread needs same-month contracts.
- **Volatility:** median 30-day annualised volatility was 31–34% across all
  four contracts in 2021–2025 and 45–57% in 2026. Brent and WTI hit their peaks
  (106% and 109%) on 17 Apr 2026, above the post-invasion spike of March–April
  2022. Heating oil's peak (115%) came on 7 Apr 2022.

## Caveats

- These are continuous front-month futures, so the roll dates add small jumps to
  daily returns and to the spreads.
- The 3-2-1 here is the standard benchmark (Cushing WTI crude against NY Harbor
  RBOB and ULSD). Real refinery margins depend on crude slate, location, yields, RINs and
  operating costs.
- Five complete years is a short sample for seasonality. "4 of 5 years" is
  suggestive, not proof.

## Open question to research

What drove 2026? Cracks, the Brent–WTI spread and volatility all broke their
2021–2025 ranges from late March 2026 onward. Before quoting this dashboard,
check the news for March–April 2026 (supply disruptions, sanctions, refinery
outages).
